# Writing Clearly

The reader should get your point in the first two sentences and be able to act without rereading. Write for a busy person who will skim.

## 1. Before you write

Answer these in a line each:

- **Who reads it?** Their role, what they already know, how much time they will give it.
- **What should they do or know afterwards?** Approve, reply by Friday, understand why the launch slipped.
- **What is the one-sentence point?** If you cannot state it, you are not ready to write. Work it out first.

If the user gave you material (notes, a thread, a report), extract the point and the facts that support it. Do not reproduce the material's order.

## 2. Lead with the point

Put the conclusion, request, or decision first. Background comes after, and only as much as the reader needs.

Before:
> Following our discussion last week, I looked into the hosting options. There are several factors to consider, including cost, reliability and support. After comparing three providers, I think we should go with Provider B.

After:
> I recommend Provider B for hosting. It is 30% cheaper than our current setup and is the only option with weekend support. Can you approve by Thursday so we can migrate before the renewal?

Patterns:

- **Request**: what you need, by when, then why.
- **Update**: status in one line (on track, at risk, blocked), then what changed, then what you need.
- **Proposal**: recommendation, cost and benefit, alternatives considered, decision needed.
- **Bad news**: the news, the impact, what you are doing about it. No warm-up paragraph.
- **Explanation**: the answer in one sentence, then how it works, then an example.

## 3. Structure

- One idea per paragraph. The first sentence of each paragraph states that idea; the rest supports it.
- Keep paragraphs to about two to four sentences in emails and messages.
- Use a list when items are parallel (steps, options, requirements). Use prose when ideas depend on each other.
- Use headings only in documents long enough to need navigation.
- Put asks and deadlines where they cannot be missed: first line, or bolded at the end, ideally both for long messages.
- Number steps when order matters. Bullet them when it does not.

## 4. Concrete over abstract

Replace vague claims with specifics the reader can check or picture.

| Abstract | Concrete |
| --- | --- |
| Performance improved significantly. | Page load dropped from 3.2 s to 1.1 s. |
| There were some issues with the rollout. | 40 users could not log in for two hours on Tuesday. |
| We should leverage synergies. | Sales can reuse the support team's FAQ for onboarding calls. |
| In the near future | By 15 November |

- Name people, systems, dates, amounts.
- Give one example for any abstract idea.
- Use active voice with a clear actor: "The script deleted the backup", not "The backup was deleted".

## 5. Cut

Delete on sight:

- Throat-clearing: "I wanted to reach out to", "I hope this finds you well" (unless the relationship calls for it), "It is worth noting that", "As you may know".
- Hedges that add nothing: "I think maybe", "sort of", "somewhat", "arguably".
- Intensifiers: "very", "really", "extremely", "incredibly".
- Doubled words: "each and every", "first and foremost", "final outcome".
- Nominalizations: "make a decision" becomes "decide", "conduct an analysis of" becomes "analyse".
- Restating the question back before answering it.
- Summaries of what you just said at the end of a short message.

Prefer short, common words: "use" (not "utilize"), "help" (not "facilitate"), "about" (not "approximately"), "now" (not "at this point in time").

## 6. Match the reader

- Expert reader: skip definitions, use the field's terms, get to specifics.
- General reader: define any term they might not know the first time, or replace it with plain words. Use an analogy only if it is accurate.
- Executive reader: decision and impact first, detail in an attachment or a final section.
- Match the register of the channel: a chat message is shorter and plainer than a formal letter. Keep the user's own voice when editing their draft; fix clarity, do not impose a style.
- Keep tone neutral and direct. Do not over-apologise or over-thank.

## 7. Length

- Messages and emails: aim for something readable without scrolling. If it is longer, ask whether part of it belongs in a linked document.
- Summaries: about one tenth of the source at most, and lead with the conclusion, not the chronology.
- If the user gives a length limit, meet it exactly.

## 8. Edit pass

After the draft, do one pass per item:

1. Read only the first two sentences. Do they carry the point and any ask?
2. Read only the first sentence of each paragraph. Does that alone tell the story?
3. Cut every sentence that would not be missed.
4. Replace each vague word with a number, name, or example.
5. Check every fact, date, name, and figure against the source material.
6. Read it as the recipient: is anything ambiguous, missing, or likely to start a back-and-forth? Answer the obvious follow-up question in advance.
7. Check the subject line (for email) states the topic and any action: "Approval needed by Thu: hosting switch to Provider B".

## Before you call it done

- [ ] The point or ask is in the first two sentences.
- [ ] Each paragraph has one idea, stated first.
- [ ] Deadlines, owners, and decisions are explicit.
- [ ] Vague claims are replaced with specifics.
- [ ] Filler, hedges, and intensifiers are gone.
- [ ] Vocabulary and length fit the reader and the channel.
- [ ] Every fact matches the source material.
- [ ] Email drafts have a subject line that states the topic and action.
